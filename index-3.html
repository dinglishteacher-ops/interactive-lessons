First Impressions

A They say it takes about seven seconds to form a first impression, and most of that time we aren't even talking. Most of us assume that our words matter most, but people tend to perceive us through our posture long before we open our mouths. Slouching may make you seem underconfident, while constantly checking your phone suggests you're absent-minded or simply not interested. The good news is that these are habits, and habits can be broken. Practising an open, relaxed posture in front of a mirror may feel silly at first, but it's one of the quickest ways to come across as more approachable.

B These days, a first impression is often made before we meet anyone at all. A potential employer, a landlord or a date will probably have looked at your profile photo and scrolled through your posts. From what they find, they will deduce all sorts of things about you, many of them wrong. A holiday photo with a drink in your hand may be perceived as fun-loving by one person and irresponsible by another. Frustratingly, you can't control who sees what, but you can make the most of your online presence by keeping it honest and up to date.

C What counts as a good first impression is not universal. People in the UK or the USA set great store by a firm handshake and direct eye contact, but in some parts of East Asia, too much eye contact can come across as aggressive. In Brazil, a stranger may greet you with a kiss on the cheek, while in Finland people tend to keep their distance until they know you well. Someone who is seen as warm and touchy-feely at home may seem rude abroad, and vice versa. It takes time to grasp these subtle distinctions, but it's well worth the effort.

D Research suggests that once we've formed a negative opinion of someone, we start looking for evidence that confirms it and ignore anything that doesn't. Psychologists call this "confirmation bias". So if a new colleague seemed conceited on their first day, we're likely to interpret their later confidence as arrogance too. Changing that impression is not impossible, but it ultimately takes many positive encounters, not just one; some studies suggest as many as eight. The lesson? It's far easier to get it right the first time than to fix it later.

E I'll never forget my first day at a new job. I'd been so nervous all morning that when I finally met my manager, I hardly said a word. I later found out she had assumed I was a cold fish who didn't want to be there. It was only months later, at an office party, that she saw the real me: chatty, laid-back and a bit of a live wire. We laughed about it, but it taught me a lesson. Nerves can easily be mistaken for unfriendliness, so these days I make a point of smiling and asking a question or two, even when I'm terrified.
