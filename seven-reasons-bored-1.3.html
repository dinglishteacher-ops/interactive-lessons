Welcome back. Now — how long could you sit in an empty room doing absolutely nothing? Ten minutes? Fifteen?

In one of the best-known studies on boredom, researchers at the University of Virginia asked volunteers to do exactly that: fifteen minutes alone with their own thoughts. No phone, no book, nothing at all. Most of them hated it, and nearly half said the experience was genuinely unpleasant.

And then came the strange part. The researchers left one more thing in the room — a button that delivered a small electric shock. The same shock those volunteers had earlier paid money to avoid. Within fifteen minutes, sixty-seven per cent of the men pressed it, and about a quarter of the women did too.

Think about that for a second. We would rather give ourselves an electric shock than sit quietly with our own minds.

Research shows that our tolerance for empty time is collapsing, and the average person now checks their phone around ninety times a day. But here's the thing. Boredom is not the enemy. It is the space where ideas appear, where problems quietly solve themselves, where you finally hear what you actually think.

So ask yourself this: when did you last give your brain fifteen empty minutes? Try it tomorrow. No phone, no music, no plan. You may find it's the most productive quarter of an hour of your week.

Seven reasons why being bored is good for you

Seven reasons why being bored is good for you

Society gets busier every year, and even when we are completely alone, we are still being stimulated by a screen and still trying to keep up with everyone else. Have we forgotten how to be bored? We treat boredom as something to escape from — but what if it is doing us good?

  1. Creativity. Boredom leads to creativity. Your best ideas rarely arrive while you are scrolling; they arrive in the shower, on a long walk, on a train with a dead phone. Only when your mind has empty space can it start building something of its own.
  2. Problem-solving. Your subconscious mind is often better at solving problems than your conscious one, because it is not following rules and procedures. But you only hear it once the noise dies down and your conscious mind has a moment to settle.
  3. Self-reflection. Being bored pushes you to think about where you actually are in life — something that racing around, permanently occupied and permanently engaged, never does. Self-reflection can be the catalyst for changing your job, your city or your whole routine.
  4. Selflessness. According to one study, bored people become more altruistic and more empathetic, and are more likely to take part in selfless activities such as volunteering, giving to charity or donating blood. Boredom, it seems, turns our attention outwards.
  5. Slowing down. The moment we feel bored, it is easy to reach for a phone or a tablet. Yet every time we favour that virtual world, we stop noticing the real one around us. Looking at a tree, a street or a sky slows the mind down and lets it enjoy the ordinary again.
  6. Breaking the cycle. Research shows we get a small dopamine hit every time we experience something new. In a world that changes by the second, we have become completely accustomed to that hit, and we chase it all day. Embrace boredom and you break the cycle — and simple pleasures start to feel like pleasures again.
  7. Taking time out. We live in a world of 24/7 connectivity. There is no downtime, and our minds are never allowed to stop. Boredom gives us time out from the constant buzz of activity. It lets us enter a calmer state and step off the roller coaster that so many of us struggle with. Is that really such a bad thing?

Writing · toolkit

Six persuasive devices

Reference
  • Personal pronouns — I, you, we. «We treat boredom as something to escape from.» Читач стає учасником, а не стороннім.
  • Rhetorical questions — питання без відповіді. «Is that really such a bad thing?» Змушує читача відповісти подумки.
  • Research, statistics, figures — «According to one study…», «Research shows…». Додає ваги твердженню.
  • Declarative sentences — короткі категоричні твердження. «Boredom leads to creativity.» Без maybe і I think.
  • Direct address & imperatives — «Embrace boredom and you break the cycle.» Звертання прямо до читача.
  • The rule of three — перелік із трьох: «volunteering, giving to charity or donating blood». Звучить повно й ритмічно.

Pronunciation · intonation

Rising or falling?

A real yes/no question goes up at the end — you want an answer. A rhetorical question goes down — you already know it. Choose, then say each one twice: once as a real question, once as a rhetorical one.

  1. "Is that really such a bad thing?" (from the article)
  2. "Have we forgotten how to be bored?" (from the article)
  3. "Did you finish the report?" (a colleague wants an answer)
  4. "When did you last give your brain fifteen empty minutes?" (from the talk)
  5. "Could you sit there for fifteen minutes?" (you're asking me, your partner)
Writing tip: a rhetorical question in your article only works if the reader can "hear" that falling tone — so make the answer obvious.

Writing · rewrite

Make it persuasive

Rewrite each flat sentence using the device in brackets. Then compare with a model version.

  1. Some people think walking is quite good for your health. (declarative sentence)
    Walking changes your health. Thirty minutes a day is all it takes.
  2. It is possible that phones are affecting people's sleep. (research / statistics)
    According to a recent study, people who use their phones in bed lose almost an hour of sleep a night.
  3. Maybe readers should try switching off their phone in the evening. (direct address / imperative)
    Switch your phone off at nine tonight and see what happens.
  4. People are busy and they do not rest enough. (personal pronouns)
    We are busier than ever, and we almost never let ourselves stop.
  5. Cooking at home has several advantages. (rule of three)
    Cooking at home saves you money, teaches you a skill and gives you back control of what you eat.
  6. Many people never take a proper holiday. (rhetorical question)
    When was the last time you took a holiday and actually switched off?

Writing · plan

Plan it in five steps

Fill in the plan before you write a single sentence.

  1. Topic. Think of it in terms of a number — odd numbers work better.
  2. Ideas. Collect more than you need — you can cut later.
  3. Title. Number + adjective + noun + promise.
  4. Style. Choose one and keep it all the way through.
  5. Notes for each point. One sentence of explanation per point, plus an example or a figure.
Structure that works
  • Title — number + promise.
  • Intro (2–3 sentences) — a problem everybody recognises, then a rhetorical question or a surprising claim.
  • Body — each point: a bold subheading (one or two words), then 2–3 sentences. Keep the pattern identical for every point.
  • Ending — one line back to the reader: a question, a challenge, or a single short sentence.
